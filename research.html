Research

Packaging and gate-drive design methods for high-current GaN power modules in 800 V data-center and EV systems.

GaN HEMTs have the best switching figure of merit among commercial power semiconductors, yet they are still missing from the converters that would gain the most from it. GaN has been confined below a few kilowatts, where a single die carries the whole load; the on-board chargers, fast chargers, and traction inverters of electric vehicles process hundreds of amperes and are still built from Si and SiC. Three barriers stand in the way: the narrow gate window of the p-GaN gate, the strong temperature dependence of the switching transient, and a fine-pitch lateral die that resists paralleling. My dissertation, Switching Modeling, Driver Technology and Packaging of GaN HEMTs in High-Current Applications, addresses each of them at the gate-drive and package level.

1. Gate-drive voltage: the partial-bootstrap gate driver

650 V p-GaN HEMT (GS-065-150) · turn-on loss vs. gate oscillation · no extra isolated supply, inductor, or control channel

An enhancement-mode p-GaN HEMT leaves the driver only a few volts between the Miller plateau and the gate's absolute maximum, so a driver fast enough to cut turn-on loss is also one that invites gate-source oscillation. The partial-bootstrap gate driver (PBGD) resolves this by stacking a self-limiting bootstrap capacitor onto the drive rail only during the Miller plateau: the voltage-fall interval that dominates turn-on loss is accelerated, while the current-rise interval that sets di/dt is left untouched, so turn-on loss and turn-on overvoltage fall together. Double-pulse tests on 650 V / 150 A devices confirm the mechanism, and a closed-loop version whose timing is triggered by the drain voltage, so that it adapts itself to load, temperature, and device size, is now in test. I also work on gate drivers for gate-injection transistors (GITs), whose p-GaN gate is held on by a steady current rather than a voltage.

2. Temperature-adaptive switching: the NTC gate-resistor array

−25 °C to 100 °C · wide-temperature transient model · series–parallel NTC array · three passive components

A GaN HEMT's threshold voltage barely moves with temperature while its transconductance falls, so the device switches more slowly the hotter it gets, the opposite of Si and SiC, whose falling threshold compensates. At the same time the crosstalk constraint that sets the gate resistance relaxes with temperature, so a fixed gate resistor sized at the cold corner wastes margin everywhere else. I built a wide-temperature transient model of the GaN half-bridge, coupling a temperature-dependent channel-current model with calibrated nonlinear junction capacitances, and used it to extract the minimum safe gate resistance at each temperature; a series–parallel array of negative-temperature-coefficient (NTC) resistors is then fitted to that trajectory by constrained optimization. From −25 °C to 100 °C the array cuts turn-on time by 30% and turn-on loss by 23% at 300 V, 120 A, and 100 °C, keeps the peak crosstalk voltage below the threshold at every operating point, and needs only three passive components.

3. Multi-chip packaging: a 650 V / 400 A GaN half-bridge module

three 650 V / 150 A dies per switch · thin-copper interface PCB · via-in-pad vertical current paths · integrated gate drivers · double-sided cooling

The largest 650 V GaN die is rated at 150 A, two to four times short of what an EV phase leg needs, so dies must be paralleled, and with nanosecond transitions, nanohenry-level mismatch decides whether they share current. Modeling showed that the mismatch in source inductance, not in gate-loop inductance, dominates the dynamic current imbalance: a fraction of a nanohenry is enough to unbalance the dies. The module answers with a thin-copper interface PCB that carries the fine-pitch die attachment and the gate drivers and connects to the heavy-copper power routing through via-in-pad vertical current paths, so the two can be optimized separately; the symmetric layout holds the source-inductance mismatch below 9 pH and the power loop measures 1.62 nH, and both faces of the module stay free for heatsinks. The module is rated 650 V / 400 A and validated at 400 V / 300 A in double-pulse tests and under continuous load with double-sided cooling.

In progress

  • Closed-loop PBGD. Experimental validation of the drain-voltage-triggered driver over temperature, gate resistance, and load current.
  • Adaptive gate resistance on a 60 A platform. Extending the NTC design into a design map over temperature and load current, with the loss saving against a fixed gate resistor quantified from measurements.
  • Three-level ANPC converter for 800 V DC buses. In a 3L-ANPC leg each device blocks 400 V, exactly the condition at which the half-bridge module is validated, so three modules form a phase leg with the high-frequency loop confined inside one module; the long commutation loop through the interconnect is the design problem. Target demonstration at 800 V / 150 A.

Where this goes

GaN's commercial success has stayed below about 3 kW, where one die carries the whole load. The high-current region, on-board chargers at 11–22 kW, DC fast chargers at 30–350 kW, and traction inverters at 100–300 kW, still belongs to SiC MOSFETs and Si IGBTs, because paralleling GaN is far less forgiving than paralleling silicon. The same requirements are now arriving from outside transportation: the 800 V DC power architectures announced for AI data centers ask GaN for the same hundreds of amperes with the same reliability, so gate-drive and packaging methods proven on EV converters carry over directly. The module and gate-drive work is part of a $5M U.S. Department of Energy Vehicle Technologies Office (VTO) project (2023–present).

  • Thermal modeling of DBC and via-in-pad structures; thermal-imaging current-sharing tests
  • Module design and assembly: interface PCB, heavy-copper PCB, DBC, via-in-pad, SMT processes
